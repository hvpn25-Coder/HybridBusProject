How the bus learns a missing traction force

01 / Purpose

Match the underlying physical force—not every noisy sample

The compensator estimates an extra signed force, called generic loss, when the model’s existing road loads do not explain the measured traction under the corresponding drive cycle.

Measured quantity

Signed net wheel traction in newtons, recorded against time. It must use the same definition and sign convention as the simulated force.

Estimated quantity

A motion-dependent correction to the modeled load. Positive means additional opposing force; negative means the model’s assumed load should be reduced.

Physical consequence

The correction changes acceleration and speed. The driver responds with different propulsion or braking effort, which changes wheel traction.

There are two distinct operations. Sensor-bias removal corrects the measurement. Generic-loss adaptation changes the vehicle dynamics. A constant sensor offset must not automatically become a physical load.

The bus is forward modeled: motor and brake forces determine acceleration, acceleration is integrated into speed, and actual speed feeds back to the driver. The desired speed is a reference; the model is not forced to follow it exactly.

02 / The complete feedback loop

Where the compensator sits

  1. Compare: subtract simulated physical traction from the bias-corrected measurement at the corresponding simulation time.
  2. Learn: filter and limit that residual, then integrate it into a signed loss estimate.
  3. Apply: fade the estimate near standstill and subtract the resulting opposing load from the vehicle’s net force.
  4. Respond: the driver reacts to the resulting speed error. Its altered motor/brake command changes actual wheel traction.
  5. Repeat: the residual decreases if the modeled closed-loop response can explain the measured force.
03 / The governing equations

One correction, used consistently in both force balances

Let L̂ be the learned estimate, L the force actually applied, and R the sum of modeled gradient, rolling and aerodynamic forces. For forward motion:

R = Fgradient + Frolling + Faerodynamic φ(v) = clamp(v / vmin, 0, 1) L = φ(v) L̂ m a = Fdrive − Ffriction − R − L + Fstandstill Fsim = m a + R + L

Above vmin, the entire estimate is applied. At zero speed, the applied loss is zero, even though the learned estimate is retained. This fade represents motion-dependent generic resistance; it is not a way to identify a static hill load while parked.

Why adding loss does not instantly add traction

Substitute the acceleration equation into the traction equation. During motion, where the standstill reaction is zero:

Fsim = [Fdrive − Ffriction − R − L] + R + L = Fdrive − Ffriction
The R and L terms cancel algebraically at an instant. Adding L at fixed motor/brake force initially changes acceleration, not net wheel traction. The increase in traction happens later because the speed-feedback driver changes wheel force. This is why the driver and actuator dynamics matter to tuning.

If the driver were disabled and wheel force held fixed, this compensator could not generally make traction track an arbitrary force recording by adjusting a load alone. It would mainly change vehicle speed. Likewise, if the motor or brake is saturated, the requested physical match may be unattainable.

What happens at a stop?

The forward-only velocity constraint prevents reverse motion. Its reaction, StandstillReaction_N, is explicitly logged. Consequently, the general identity is Fsim = Fdrive − Ffriction + Fstandstill. The ideal constraint switches abruptly, so small differences in stop timing can create large single-sample force residuals. The compensator suspends learning near rest to avoid treating these events as persistent resistance.

04 / Inside Traction_Compensator

From noisy residual to a usable estimate

Fmeas(t) = Fraw(t) − b e(t) = Fmeas(t) − Fsim(t) q(t) = 1 when v > vmin and tstart ≤ t ≤ tend; otherwise 0 u(t) = clamp(q e, −ErrorClip, +ErrorClip) FilterTau × dz/dt + z = u D(z) = sign(z) × max(|z| − Deadband, 0) dx/dt = clamp(Enable × q × D(z) / Tau, −MaxRate, +MaxRate) x limited to [−Limit, +Limit]; x(0) = InitialForce L̂ = Enable × x

The limited integrator stops outward integration at its bounds and can move inward when the error reverses. MaxRate bounds changes in the estimate; applied force can change faster during the low-speed fade because φ(v) changes too.

Sensor bias versus generic loss

If the raw sensor reads +1,100 N during a confirmed level-road, zero-net-traction stop, subtracting that calibrated bias is appropriate. Applying +1,100 N as a permanent mechanical load would instead make the driver work against a sensor error. Conversely, a real missing resistance while moving belongs in the physical loss estimate, not in a blanket sensor offset.

The generic runner uses either Bias_N, an explicitly supplied StationaryWindow, or zero bias if neither is provided. A stationary window uses a robust trimmed mean: samples beyond six robust standard deviations from the median are excluded. The profile must indicate near-zero target speed and grade; measured speed is checked too when supplied. Actual parked/zero-traction validity still depends on the recording context.

05 / A numerical illustration

What a positive 1,000 N mismatch does

  1. Initial comparison: suppose the debiased measurement requires 11,000 N while the model currently generates 10,000 N. The positive residual is +1,000 N.
  2. Load estimate increases: filtered positive residual increases L̂. Assume L has eventually increased by 1,000 N, with the vehicle moving above the fade threshold.
  3. Immediate plant response: at unchanged wheel force, a 20,000 kg vehicle’s acceleration changes by −1,000 / 20,000 = −0.05 m/s².
  4. Driver response: the vehicle begins to fall below the speed reference. The driver requests more propulsion, or less braking as appropriate, subject to available actuator capability.
  5. New balance: if wheel force rises by about 1,000 N, acceleration can return near its previous value. Simulated traction is now closer to the measured requirement, so further adaptation slows.

A negative mismatch reverses this reasoning: the estimated correction decreases, assumed opposing load is reduced, and the driver ultimately reduces propulsion or increases braking. The sign refers to a correction to the assumed model; it does not imply negative energy dissipation by a real rolling-resistance element.

06 / Generic case configuration

Two timescales: offline selection, causal simulation

Before the final run: MATLAB case runner

Reads the full recording, validates profiles, calibrates optional bias, estimates noise, scales defaults, runs candidate simulations and chooses an acceptable setting.

During each simulation: Simulink blocks

Use the current measurement/model residual and retained states to estimate load causally. The filter and gain parameters stay fixed for that candidate run.

The setup is reusable across supported vehicle configurations, but it is not a continuously self-retuning controller. The full-recording setup must be repeated for a new case or a materially different sensor-noise regime.

How the initial settings are scaled

Δt = median(diff(measurement time)) σfast ≈ 1.4826 × MAD(diff(Fraw)) / √2 Fpeak = MotorCount × MotorPeakTorque × GearRatio × GearEfficiency / WheelRadius Favailable = min(Fpeak, MotorCount × MotorPower × GearEfficiency / max(vtypical, 1 m/s)) Tdriver = Mass / (DriverKp × Favailable) Tbase = max(0.5 s, Tdriver, 5 × MotorLag, 3 × BrakeLag) Limit = min(0.03 × m × g, 0.25 × Fpeak) FilterTau = max(5 × Δt, 0.2 × Tbase) Deadband = max(10⁻⁶ × m × g, σ × √[Δt / (2 × FilterTau + Δt)]) ErrorClip = max(8 × σ, 0.6 × Limit) MaxRate = 0.3 × Limit / Tbase Candidate Tau = {0.75, 1.5, 3} × Tbase

MAD here is median absolute deviation about the median. When a stationary window is supplied, σ is the larger of the fast-difference estimate and the robust stationary spread. NoiseSigma_N and ForceLimit_N can override the corresponding defaults. The representative speed is the median desired speed above 1 km/h, converted to m/s; it defaults to 1 m/s when no such samples exist. These formulas are engineering heuristics, not a derivation of universal optimal gains.

Acceptance is based on unsmoothed physical residuals

  • A disabled-compensation baseline is simulated first, with the same measurement bias treatment.
  • Both time regions need at least 20 moving samples. The candidate must improve training-region RMS and avoid unacceptable degradation in the later region.
  • Speed RMS must stay below the configured tolerance and must not exceed baseline speed RMS by more than max(0.5 km/h, 25% of baseline speed RMS).
  • Fewer than 5% of moving samples may have an estimate magnitude at or above 98% of its force limit.
  • If measured speed is available, simulated-versus-measured speed RMS is also checked against the speed tolerance.
  • The accepted candidate with the lowest training-region RMS is returned. If none qualifies, compensation is disabled and the reason is reported.
This is not independent predictive validation. Both intervals come from the same recording, and the second interval participates in acceptance. A new vehicle recording is still needed to establish performance beyond the calibration case. AutoTune=false evaluates one nominal candidate; it does not bypass acceptance checks.

Understand report.status

  • accepted: a candidate passed the implemented comparison and constraint checks.
  • disabled_no_accepted_candidate: no candidate demonstrated acceptable improvement.
  • disabled_insufficient_moving_data: there was not enough moving data in both time regions.
  • disabled_speed_tracking_inadequate: the baseline trajectory failed the speed criterion.

With the default Apply=true, the returned configuration is saved into the model—including a disabled configuration if acceptance fails. Use Apply=false for experiments that must not change the saved model.

09 / Interpretation and limits

A better force match is not proof of the cause

The compensator identifies a lumped discrepancy. A single force trace cannot uniquely separate wrong rolling resistance, drag, grade, mass, speed mismatch, actuator error or sensor drift. A fitted loss can absorb several of them. Check synchronized speed, known grade, vehicle specifications and sensor calibration before interpreting the estimate as a real additional resistance.

Within the current model’s scope

Forward-only longitudinal travel; existing PI driver; no tire slip; reduced-order electric drive and pneumatic brake response; motion-dependent signed load correction.

Requires additional modeling or data

Reverse driving, tire slip, battery SOC/thermal limits, persistent static-load identification, changing sensor bias, unknown signal delays, different force definitions, or a different control architecture.

When a match looks poor

  1. Check the comparison: use debiased measurement and physical simulated traction, in N, on the same time base.
  2. Check speed: a force trace from another speed/acceleration history is not a valid resistance target for this run.
  3. Check the residual’s character: fast noise needs conditioning; a slow systematic residual may need load adaptation or model correction.
  4. Check limits: force or rate saturation indicates that the configured correction cannot keep up or that the required change is too large.
  5. Check stops separately: zero-speed constraints and fading can create transient mismatch; they should not drive a permanent learned load.
  6. Check report.status: a disabled result is an assessment outcome, not a successful fitted model.
What “matched” means here: the physical simulation generates traction closer to a calibrated measurement while retaining consistent force equations and acceptable speed behavior. It does not mean zero error, exact noise reproduction, unique resistance identification, or guaranteed operation under every condition.
